Lecture 13

Clifford circuits: simulable, and what they lack

What this lecture does

Lecture 12 built codes from commuting Paulis. This lecture asks: which circuits can a classical computer simulate, and what does a protected computation need beyond them? Circuits of HHH, SSS and CNOT, started on ∣0⋯0⟩\ket{0\cdots0}∣0⋯0⟩ and read by Pauli measurements, can be followed through nnn Pauli strings and simulated classically (Theorem 13.4), although they make entangled states. What they lack is TTT, which injection gets by consuming the state ∣A⟩=T∣+⟩\magic=T\ket{+}∣A⟩=T∣+⟩ (Theorem 13.6). The threshold theorem (cited) says what a computation needs to survive noise everywhere. This is the first half of the third answer: where the advantage lives.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Compute how HHH, SSS and CNOT act on Paulis by conjugation, and follow the generators of a stabilizer state through Clifford gates and Pauli measurements, signs included.
  • State the Gottesman–Knill theorem with its hypotheses, and sketch its proof.
  • Prove that injection applies TTT, up to a global phase, by consuming ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩, and show that ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩ is not a stabilizer state.
  • State the threshold theorem with its hypotheses.

13.1 The Clifford group

Definition 13.1 (The Clifford group). A gate UUU on nnn qubits is a Clifford if UEU†∈GnUEU^\dagger\in\Pauli{n}UEU†∈Gn​ for every E∈GnE\in\Pauli{n}E∈Gn​. They form the Clifford group.

It is a group: conjugation by a Clifford maps the finite set Gn\Pauli{n}Gn​ one-to-one, so onto, itself; so the inverse is a Clifford. Conjugation respects products, and as Y=iXZY=iXZY=iXZ every element of Gn\Pauli{n}Gn​ is a phase times a product of XqX_qXq​’s and ZqZ_qZq​’s; so the images of the XqX_qXq​ and ZqZ_qZq​ decide whether a gate is a Clifford. Multiplying the matrices, HXH†=Z,HZH†=X,SXS†=Y,SZS†=Z,\begin{aligned} HXH^\dagger&=Z, & HZH^\dagger&=X,\\ SXS^\dagger&=Y, & SZS^\dagger&=Z, \end{aligned}HXH†SXS†​=Z,=Y,​HZH†SZS†​=X,=Z,​ and the CNOT, with qubit 1 its control, maps X⊗1↦X⊗X,1⊗X↦1⊗X,Z⊗1↦Z⊗1,1⊗Z↦Z⊗Z\begin{aligned} X\otimes\Id&\mapsto X\otimes X, & \Id\otimes X&\mapsto\Id\otimes X,\\ Z\otimes\Id&\mapsto Z\otimes\Id, & \Id\otimes Z&\mapsto Z\otimes Z \end{aligned}X⊗1Z⊗1​↦X⊗X,↦Z⊗1,​1⊗X1⊗Z​↦1⊗X,↦Z⊗Z​ (Nielsen and Chuang, Fig. 10.7, p. 460). In circuits, an XXX on the control before a CNOT is an XXX on both wires after it, (13.1) an equality of matrices; likewise a ZZZ on the target becomes ZZZ on both. So circuits of HHH, SSS and CNOT are Cliffords, and so are the Pauli gates, Z=S2Z=S^{2}Z=S2 and X=HS2HX=HS^{2}HX=HS2H.

Theorem 13.2 (Generation cited). Every Clifford on nnn qubits is, up to a global phase, a circuit of O(n2)O(n^{2})O(n2) gates HHH, SSS and CNOT.

The count is unconditional (Nielsen and Chuang, Theorem 10.6, pp. 461–462). The phase cannot be dropped: eiπ/81e^{i\pi/8}\Ideiπ/81 fixes every Pauli, but on one qubit every product of HHH and SSS has a power of iii as its determinant (det⁡H=−1\det H=-1detH=−1, det⁡S=i\det S=idetS=i), and det⁡(eiπ/81)=eiπ/4\det(e^{i\pi/8}\Id)=e^{i\pi/4}det(eiπ/81)=eiπ/4 is none. And TTT is no Clifford: TXT†=(X+Y)/2TXT^\dagger=(X+Y)/\sqrt2TXT†=(X+Y)/2​ is no phase times a Pauli. Only TTT and T†T^\daggerT† of the alphabet (Definition 6.8) lie outside.

13.2 Gottesman–Knill

A gate changes the Paulis that fix a state only by renaming them: if gψ=ψg\psi=\psigψ=ψ, then (UgU†)Uψ=Uψ(UgU^\dagger)U\psi=U\psi(UgU†)Uψ=Uψ. This is the whole idea.

By Theorem 12.3, a stabilizer group with nnn independent generators on nnn qubits fixes a space of dimension 20=12^{0}=120=1: one state, up to a global phase. It is a stabilizer state, and its generators, nnn strings with their signs, determine it. The state ∣x⟩\ket{x}∣x⟩ is one, with the generators (−1)x1Z1,…,(−1)xnZn(-1)^{x_1}Z_1,\dots,(-1)^{x_n}Z_n(−1)x1​Z1​,…,(−1)xn​Zn​. If ψ\psiψ is the stabilizer state of g1,…,gng_1,\dots,g_ng1​,…,gn​ and UUU is a Clifford, then UψU\psiUψ is the stabilizer state of Ug1U†,…,UgnU†Ug_1U^\dagger,\dots,Ug_nU^\daggerUg1​U†,…,Ugn​U†: conjugation is one-to-one and respects products, so these again commute, are independent and generate a group without −1-\Id−1. Each generator is a string of nnn letters with a sign, 2n+12n+12n+1 bits, and a gate on one or two qubits changes, in each generator, the letters on those qubits and perhaps the sign, by the table of §13.1: O(n)O(n)O(n) steps for all nnn.

A measurement of g=±Pg=\pm Pg=±P, for a Pauli string P≠1P\neq\IdP=1, is the projective measurement 12(1+g)\tfrac12(\Id+g)21​(1+g), 12(1−g)\tfrac12(\Id-g)21​(1−g); the outcome bbb is the eigenvalue (−1)b(-1)^{b}(−1)b of ggg, and the circuit of §12.3 performs it. Reading qubit qqq is the case g=Zqg=Z_qg=Zq​.

Lemma 13.3 (Measuring a stabilizer state). Let ψ\psiψ be the stabilizer state of g1,…,gng_1,\dots,g_ng1​,…,gn​, and measure g=±Pg=\pm Pg=±P.

  1. If ggg anticommutes with a generator, say g1g_1g1​, replace each other gjg_jgj​ that anticommutes with ggg by g1gjg_1g_jg1​gj​. Each outcome has probability 12\tfrac1221​, and the outcome bbb leaves the stabilizer state of (−1)bg,g2,…,gn(-1)^{b}g,g_2,\dots,g_n(−1)bg,g2​,…,gn​.

It leaves ψb=(1+(−1)bg)ψ/2\psi_b=(\Id+(-1)^{b}g)\psi/\sqrt2ψb​=(1+(−1)bg)ψ/2​, fixed by (−1)bg(-1)^{b}g(−1)bg, as g2=1g^{2}=\Idg2=1, and by gjg_jgj​ for j≥2j\ge2j≥2, which commutes with ggg and fixes ψ\psiψ. To find ccc, write each string as two rows of nnn bits, its XXX-part and its ZZZ-part, YYY counting in both. The bits aja_jaj​ with h=g1a1⋯gnanh=g_1^{a_1}\cdots g_n^{a_n}h=g1a1​​⋯gnan​​ solve 2n2n2n linear equations modulo 222, the parts of ggg’s string as sums of the generators’ parts, and elimination solves them in O(n3)O(n^{3})O(n3) steps. Multiplying those generators, phases included, gives hhh, and comparing it with ggg gives ccc. Nielsen and Chuang (p. 463) and de Wolf (p. 176) leave this step out.

Theorem 13.4 (Gottesman–Knill sketched). Let a circuit on nnn qubits start in ∣0⋯0⟩\ket{0\cdots0}∣0⋯0⟩ and take mmm steps, each an HHH, an SSS, a CNOT or a measurement of a Pauli ±P\pm P±P, where the choice of each step may depend on the earlier outcomes through a classical polynomial-time rule. A classical algorithm with coins, in time polynomial in nnn and mmm, gives outcomes with the circuit’s joint probabilities, and the generators of the state the circuit leaves.

Sketch. Start from Z1,…,ZnZ_1,\dots,Z_nZ1​,…,Zn​. A gate updates the generators by the table, in O(n)O(n)O(n) steps. A measurement is Lemma 13.3: in case (i) a coin gives bbb, and the update takes O(n2)O(n^{2})O(n2) steps, at most nnn products of strings; case (ii) takes the elimination, O(n3)O(n^{3})O(n3) steps. After each step the generators are those of the circuit’s state, given the outcomes so far, and each outcome has the probability the circuit gives it then; so the outcomes have the circuit’s joint probabilities, the products of these. In all, O(mn3)O(mn^{3})O(mn3) steps, and the rule’s. 

The theorem is unconditional. The same algorithm starts from any stabilizer state given by its generators. The Pauli gates are circuits of HHH and SSS, and readings of qubits are measurements of ZqZ_qZq​, so both are covered. The algorithm samples: it gives outcomes with the right probabilities, the form of de Wolf’s Theorem 6 (pp. 175–176), and the theorem says nothing about computing amplitudes. Nielsen and Chuang state it as Theorem 10.7 (p. 464); Gottesman (p. 52) credits Knill.

The input hypothesis matters. Add copies of ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩ as inputs, and §13.4 turns each into a TTT; then the circuits of the theorem reach every circuit over the alphabet of Definition 6.8. A simulation as in the theorem for them would put BQP\BQPBQP inside BPP\BPPBPP. None is known, and BQP≠BPP\BQP\neq\BPPBQP=BPP is a conjecture (Lecture 6).

13.4 Injection

13.5 Threshold

Lecture 10 took the encoding, the syndrome circuits and the corrections to be exact. They fail too. Call each preparation of ∣0⟩\ket{0}∣0⟩, each gate, each qubit resting for one step and each measurement a location.

Theorem 13.7 (Threshold cited). Suppose that

  1. each location fails independently, with probability at most ϵ\epsilonϵ; a failure may apply any operation to the location’s qubits, and otherwise the location is exact;

  2. gates act in parallel, fresh qubits in ∣0⟩\ket{0}∣0⟩ can be brought in at every step, the inputs are ∣0⟩\ket{0}∣0⟩, and the classical processing of outcomes is exact;

  3. fault-tolerant gadgets are given: encoded versions of the locations, with error correction on their blocks, substituted for the locations level by level; if a gadget, with the error corrections before it, contains at most one fault, then decoding its output ideally gives what the ideal location gives on the decoded input.

There is an ϵ0>0\epsilon_0>0ϵ0​>0, fixed by the gadgets, such that if ϵ<ϵ0\epsilon<\epsilon_0ϵ<ϵ0​, then for every δ>0\delta>0δ>0 every circuit with LLL locations is simulated by a noisy circuit with L⋅polylog(L/δ)L\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(L/\delta)L⋅polylog(L/δ) locations, whose output probabilities differ from the ideal ones by at most δ\deltaδ in all.

This is Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill’s Theorem 1 (p. 13; hypotheses pp. 2, 11 and 13–14), unconditional under (a)–(c), with the dependence on δ\deltaδ as Aharonov and Ben-Or state it (Theorem 10, p. 52). The encoding, syndrome circuits and corrections are locations like any other, and below ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​ a computation of any length is still simulated within δ\deltaδ, at a polylogarithmic cost: this answers Lecture 10. A code of distance 333 corrects one error in a block, but one faulty gate can make two; the gadgets of (c) keep one fault harmless, and independence makes two faults in one gadget rare, of order ϵ2\epsilon^{2}ϵ2. Even storage needs parallel error correction, and a constant supply of fresh ancillas is necessary (Nielsen and Chuang, p. 494). With noisy classical processing the error stays of order ϵ\epsilonϵ, however large the codes (Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill, p. 14); Aharonov and Ben-Or need no classical processing during the computation (p. 1).

Their gadgets for data act on encoded blocks by Clifford operations, and obtain TTT by this lecture’s injection circuit, consuming an encoded ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩ that a further gadget prepares (Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill, Fig. 9 and p. 28): a protected computation needs, beyond Clifford circuits, a supply of magic states.

Values of ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​ depend on the noise model and the gadgets, and are not comparable: 2.73×10−52.73\times10^{-5}2.73×10−5 for Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill’s gadgets under their faults (Theorem 3, p. 38), and about 10−610^{-6}10−6 for Aharonov and Ben-Or’s best case, which assumes error-free classical operations (p. 52). None is the threshold.

13.6 Where the advantage lives

Clifford circuits make entangled states, the Bell state of Example 13.5 among them, and Theorem 13.4 simulates them all. So entanglement alone does not put a computation beyond a classical computer’s reach; in Nielsen and Chuang’s words (p. 464), “some quantum computations involving highly entangled states may be simulated efficiently on classical computers”. What the simulated circuits lack is a gate outside the Clifford group, TTT, or the state ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩ that injection turns into it. If BQP≠BPP\BQP\neq\BPPBQP=BPP, which is a conjecture, the difference needs one of them: this is where the advantage lives (de Wolf, p. 175). Protection does not change this: a protected computation still consumes magic states. Lecture 14 asks whether protection can grow with the size of the system.

Summary

  • Cliffords: HHH, SSS and CNOT map Paulis to Paulis and generate the Clifford group up to a global phase (cited); TTT lies outside.
  • Gottesman–Knill (sketched, unconditional): from ∣0⋯0⟩\ket{0\cdots0}∣0⋯0⟩, Clifford gates, Pauli measurements and classical control are simulated by sampling, through nnn Pauli strings.
  • Injection: a CNOT, a reading and a classically controlled SSS apply TTT, up to a global phase, consuming ∣A⟩\magic∣A⟩, which is no stabilizer state.
  • Threshold (cited; unconditional): independent faults below ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​, parallel gates, fresh qubits, exact classical processing and gadgets give computations of any length at a polylogarithmic cost.

6 Which is the threshold theorem, with its hypotheses, as Lecture 13 states it (cited)?

Here is the hint — try once more. Which parts of a protected computation may fail in the theorem, and what must the gadgets do with one fault? The theorem does not need exact corrections: it lets every location fail, the encoding, the syndrome circuits and the corrections included, which answers Lecture 10's question; the failures are independent, may apply any operation, and occur with probability below ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​. It needs parallel gates, fresh qubits and exact classical processing, and gadgets that, with at most one fault, the error corrections before them included, still act as the ideal location after ideal decoding. A code of distance 333 corrects one error in a block, not one fault: one faulty two-qubit gate can leave two errors in a block, which is why the gadgets are a hypothesis. And ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​ depends on the noise model and the gadgets: 2.73×10−52.73\times10^{-5}2.73×10−5 for Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill's gadgets, about 10−610^{-6}10−6 for Aharonov and Ben-Or's best case; no value is the threshold for every model.
