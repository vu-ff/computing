Lecture 2

Qubits and interference

Lecture 1 computed with bits, sure or random, by products of matrices in sequence and Kronecker products in parallel. This lecture keeps those rules and makes the entries complex amplitudes; a probability becomes the squared modulus of a sum of them. It asks: what changes when probabilities become amplitudes? The path sum (Theorem 2.3) answers: an amplitude is a sum over paths through the circuit, the squared paths alone give a probabilistic circuit of Lecture 1, and the rest is interference. Its first visible consequence is Deutsch’s problem, which one quantum query solves and one classical query cannot.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Translate Griffiths’ spin 12\tfrac1221​ into qubits, gates and readings, and compute the probabilities of a reading.
  • Read a circuit of gates as one unitary matrix, and its mirror image, with adjoint boxes, as the inverse.
  • Write an output amplitude as a sum over paths, and split its probability into the part a probabilistic circuit gives and the interference.
  • Solve Deutsch’s problem with one query by phase kickback, prove that one classical query cannot, and say why the control qubit changes.

2.2 Soundness

Theorem 2.2 (Soundness). For complex matrices, the interchange law (A⊗B)(C⊗D)=AC⊗BD(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD(A⊗B)(C⊗D)=AC⊗BD holds whenever ACACAC and BDBDBD are defined, and (AB)†=B†A†,(A⊗B)†=A†⊗B†. \begin{gathered} (AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger,\\ (A\otimes B)^\dagger=A^\dagger\otimes B^\dagger. \end{gathered} (AB)†=B†A†,(A⊗B)†=A†⊗B†.​(2.3) Hence, for a circuit drawn without crossings, each box on neighbouring wires:

  1. it has one matrix, however its diagram is cut into columns;

  2. a part of it that fills a rectangle, some neighbouring wires between two cuts with no box leaving it, may be replaced by any diagram with the same matrix, and the matrix of the whole does not change;

  3. its mirror image, with each box UUU replaced by U†U^\daggerU†, has the adjoint matrix. So a circuit of gates is unitary, and its mirror image with adjoint boxes is its inverse.

Proof. The interchange law is Theorem 1.3, whose proof only multiplies and adds entries, and so holds over C\CC as written. For the adjoints, entry by entry, with the labels of Definition 1.2, ((AB)†)ik=∑jAkjBji‾=∑j(B†)ij(A†)jk,\begin{aligned} \bigl((AB)^\dagger\bigr)_{ik} &=\overline{\textstyle\sum_jA_{kj}B_{ji}}\\ &=\textstyle\sum_j(B^\dagger)_{ij}(A^\dagger)_{jk}, \end{aligned}((AB)†)ik​​=∑j​Akj​Bji​​=∑j​(B†)ij​(A†)jk​,​ and the entry of (A⊗B)†(A\otimes B)^\dagger(A⊗B)† in row (i,i′)(i,i')(i,i′) and column (j,j′)(j,j')(j,j′) is AjiBj′i′‾=(A†)ij(B†)i′j′\overline{A_{ji}B_{j'i'}}=(A^\dagger)_{ij}(B^\dagger)_{i'j'}Aji​Bj′i′​​=(A†)ij​(B†)i′j′​. Then (i) is the argument of §1.3. For (ii), cut at the sides of the rectangle. Before the replacement and after it, each column between the cuts is Rt⊗Mt⊗Rt′R_t\otimes M_t\otimes R'_tRt​⊗Mt​⊗Rt′​, with MtM_tMt​ the part’s boxes and RtR_tRt​, Rt′R'_tRt′​ the boxes above and below it, and by the interchange law these columns multiply to R⊗M⊗R′R\otimes M\otimes R'R⊗M⊗R′, where MMM is the matrix of the part. For (iii), the adjoint of a product KT⋯K1K_T\cdots K_1KT​⋯K1​ of columns is K1†⋯KT†K_1^\dagger\cdots K_T^\daggerK1†​⋯KT†​, and each Kt†K_t^\daggerKt†​ is the Kronecker product of the adjoint boxes: the columns of the mirror image, read left to right. Last, (U⊗V)†(U⊗V)=U†U⊗V†V=1(U\otimes V)^\dagger(U\otimes V)=U^\dagger U\otimes V^\dagger V=\Id(U⊗V)†(U⊗V)=U†U⊗V†V=1, so each column of gates is unitary, and so is their product. 

These are cases of a general theorem. Complex matrices with ⊗\otimes⊗ and †\dagger† form a dagger symmetric monoidal category, and in any such category two circuit diagrams with the same boxes wired the same way, crossings allowed, denote the same morphism, and the dagger of a diagram is its mirror image (Selinger, Thm 3.12, after Joyal and Street, and §7.2).

The twin’s probability of yyy is the sum of the squared paths. The quantum probability adds the cross terms of (2.5), the interference. Their sum is real, since the terms for (p,q)(p,q)(p,q) and (q,p)(q,p)(q,p) are conjugate, and it can be negative. A register of the twin already takes 2n2^{n}2n numbers to describe, as Lecture 1’s foil showed, so the count is not the difference; the cross terms are.

A run of the circuit returns one string yyy, drawn with the probability (2.5). The sum over 2n(T−1)2^{n(T-1)}2n(T−1) paths is how that probability is computed, not something the circuit evaluates and hands over: no run reads all the paths at once. What the paths can do is cancel or reinforce each other before the one string is drawn.

Proposition 2.7 (One classical query). Every classical procedure that queries f ⁣:{0,1}→{0,1}f\colon\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}f:{0,1}→{0,1} once, deterministic or with random bits, outputs f(0)⊕f(1)f(0)\oplus f(1)f(0)⊕f(1) correctly with probability at most 12\tfrac1221​ for at least one of the four fff.

Proof. Fix the random bits. The procedure then presents a fixed string (x,y)(x,y)(x,y), receives y⊕f(x)y\oplus f(x)y⊕f(x), and answers with a function of f(x)f(x)f(x) alone. The two fff that agree at xxx differ in f(0)⊕f(1)f(0)\oplus f(1)f(0)⊕f(1), so it is right for exactly one of each pair, that is, for two of the four fff. Its success averaged over the four fff is therefore 12\tfrac1221​ for every value of the random bits, so its probability of success averaged over the four fff is 12\tfrac1221​, and for some fff that probability is at most 12\tfrac1221​. 

A coin toss, with no query, also succeeds with probability 12\tfrac1221​, and two classical queries always succeed: one classical query is worth no more than none, and one quantum query suffices. This advantage is shown here, not explained: Lecture 5 sets up the query model in which such counts are compared, and finds there a gap that grows exponentially with the number of input bits, a separation relative to an oracle.

2.6 The control guard

Example 2.8 (QQ: the control changes). For f(x)=xf(x)=xf(x)=x, the oracle Uf∣x⟩∣y⟩=∣x⟩∣y⊕x⟩U_f\ket{x}\ket{y}=\ket{x}\ket{y\oplus x}Uf​∣x⟩∣y⟩=∣x⟩∣y⊕x⟩ is the CNOT of Definition 1.7. By the kickback on each term of ∣+⟩\ket{+}∣+⟩, (2.8) an equality of vectors. The control enters as ∣+⟩\ket{+}∣+⟩ and leaves as ∣−⟩\ket{-}∣−⟩; the target is unchanged.

Lecture 1’s rule that controls are read and left unchanged is a statement about basis states. On a superposition the control can change, and the kickback is exactly that change. Phase estimation (Lecture 7) is built on it: there the phase lands on the control register.

Summary

  • Handed on: qubits, gates and readings (Definition 2.1); a circuit has one unitary matrix, and its mirror image with adjoint boxes is the inverse (Theorem 2.2).
  • The main result: an amplitude is a sum over paths; the probabilistic twin gives the sum of the squared paths, and the rest is interference (Theorem 2.3 and Example 2.4).
  • Kickback: on ∣−⟩\ket{-}∣−⟩ the oracle puts (−1)f(x)(-1)^{f(x)}(−1)f(x) on the query register; one query solves Deutsch’s problem, and one classical query does no better than a coin (Example 2.6 and Proposition 2.7).
  • The guard: on ∣+⟩∣−⟩\ket{+}\ket{-}∣+⟩∣−⟩ the control of a CNOT changes (Example 2.8); Lecture 7 uses it.
  • Next: Lecture 3, the state of part of a register.
