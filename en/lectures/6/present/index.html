For ∣101⟩\ket{101}∣101⟩ and ∣011⟩\ket{011}∣011⟩: The Toffoli sends 011011011 to 111111111 and fixes 101101101; U~\tilde UU~ acts on wire 2 when wires 1 and 3 hold 111, so on ∣101⟩\ket{101}∣101⟩ and ∣111⟩\ket{111}∣111⟩. 6.3 The control-phase guard

A gate’s global phase changes nothing (Theorem 3.7). Under a control it does.

For U=1U=\IdU=1 and α=π\alpha=\piα=π: C(1)=1\Ctl{\Id}=\IdC(1)=1, while C(−1)=Z⊗1\Ctl{-\Id}=Z\otimes\IdC(−1)=Z⊗1; with the control in ∣+⟩\ket{+}∣+⟩, HHH and a reading of the control give 000 in the first case and 111 in the second, with certainty.

So a gate compiled up to a global phase may not be put under a control: to control UUU, compile C(U)\Ctl{U}C(U) itself, a gate on one more qubit. Lecture 7 controls powers of a gate whose eigenphase is the answer.

6.4 The circuit model, BQP\BQPBQP, BPP\BPPBPP, amplification

A quantum algorithm handles inputs of every length, so it is a rule that writes a circuit for each. The rule is a classical algorithm, which we take as known (a program, without coins); it runs in polynomial time if, for some polynomial ppp, it stops after at most p(n)p(n)p(n) steps on every input of length nnn. A decision problem is a function a ⁣:{0,1}∗→{0,1}a\colon\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}a:{0,1}∗→{0,1} on all strings; a(x)a(x)a(x) is the answer on the input xxx.

Definition 6.8 (The circuit model; BQP\BQPBQP, BPP\BPPBPP). The alphabet is {H,S,T,CNOT}\{H,S,T,\CNOT\}{H,S,T,CNOT} with the inverses S†S^\daggerS† and T†T^\daggerT†.

  1. A decision problem aaa is in BQP\BQPBQP if a classical algorithm, given xxx, writes in polynomial time a circuit CxC_xCx​ over the alphabet such that CxC_xCx​, run on ∣0⋯0⟩\ket{0\cdots0}∣0⋯0⟩ with its top wire read, gives a(x)a(x)a(x) with probability at least 23\tfrac2332​.

  2. It is in BPP\BPPBPP if a classical algorithm, given xxx, writes in polynomial time a circuit DxD_xDx​ of AND, NOT and FAN gates (Definition 1.1) with kkk inputs and one output, such that Dx(r)=a(x)D_x(r)=a(x)Dx​(r)=a(x) for at least 23\tfrac2332​ of the strings r∈{0,1}kr\in\{0,1\}^{k}r∈{0,1}k, its coins.

  3. It is in P\PtimeP if a classical algorithm computes a(x)a(x)a(x) in polynomial time, and in PSPACE\PSPACEPSPACE if one computes it with memory polynomial in the length of xxx, in any time.

This is the usual BPP\BPPBPP: a polynomial-time algorithm that tosses coins, run on xxx and written out step by step, is such a circuit in its coins (Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi, Def. 4.2, Thm 2.3). The alphabet is finite on purpose: if the algorithm could name any gate with computable entries, the gate equal to XXX when a(x)=1a(x)=1a(x)=1 and to 1\Id1 otherwise would decide aaa alone, the work hidden in its entries.

Classical gates are exact. CNOT is in the alphabet, X=HS2HX=HS^{2}HX=HS2H, and the Toffoli is (Nielsen and Chuang, Fig. 4.9) (6.2) an equality of matrices.

The second: the classical run reads each coin when it is tossed, and later gates use the outcome; here, read wires 2 and 3 after the Hadamards, then apply XXX to the top wire if both read 111. The Toffoli uses wires 2 and 3 only as controls, so by Theorem 4.5 the readings move to the end with the same joint statistics, the use Example 4.6 announced. When the coins are read, or whether they are, changes nothing in the answer’s statistics.

6.7 The first answer

The course asks what a quantum computation is, and what makes the difference visible. Its first answer: a quantum computation is a family of circuits over a finite universal gate set, written by a classical polynomial-time algorithm, run on ∣0⋯0⟩\ket{0\cdots0}∣0⋯0⟩ and measured. BQP\BQPBQP is the class of decision problems such computations solve with bounded error, and it is robust: neither the threshold, strictly between 12\tfrac1221​ and 111, nor gate errors with a small sum, nor other gates on a few qubits with efficiently computable entries change it (§§6.1, 6.4).

What is proved: BPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE\BPP\subseteq\BQP\subseteq\PSPACEBPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE. The 2m2^{m}2m amplitudes of a register do not take a computation beyond PSPACE\PSPACEPSPACE: the path sum adds them up in polynomial memory. Whether they take it beyond BPP\BPPBPP is open; Lecture 5’s separation holds relative to an oracle. The next lectures look for the difference: Lecture 8 a problem, factoring, that a quantum computer solves in polynomial time with bounded error and no known classical algorithm does, and Lecture 9 a query problem with a proved bound on each side.

Summary

  • Errors add: every outcome probability moves by at most 2∑t∥Ut−Vt∥2\sum_t\norm{U_t-V_t}2∑t​∥Ut​−Vt​∥ (Theorem 6.2).
  • Universality (cited): HHH and TTT approximate every one-qubit gate up to a global phase, with O(log⁡c(1/ϵ))O(\log^{c}(1/\epsilon))O(logc(1/ϵ)) gates (Theorems 6.5 and 6.6); under a control the phase counts (Example 6.7).
  • The model: BQP\BQPBQP and BPP\BPPBPP are decision classes, with circuits written in polynomial time; a vote amplifies 23\tfrac2332​ to 1−δ1-\delta1−δ (Definition 6.8 and Proposition 6.9).
  • The main result: BPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE\BPP\subseteq\BQP\subseteq\PSPACEBPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE (Theorems 6.11 and 6.12).
