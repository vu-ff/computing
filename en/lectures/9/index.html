Lecture 9

Search, and why it cannot be faster

What this lecture does

Lecture 8 found factors, with no classical lower bound to compare. This lecture asks: how much faster than classical can unstructured search be, and could it be faster still? Grover’s algorithm finds one marked item among NNN with about π4N\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N4π​N​ queries (Theorem 9.1); a classical algorithm needs at least 2N/3−12N/3-12N/3−1 (Proposition 9.2). The main result is the floor: every quantum algorithm needs at least (2N/3−1)/2(\sqrt{2N/3}-1)/2(2N/3​−1)/2 (Theorem 9.5). With a bound proved on each side comes the course’s second answer.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Prove that Grover’s iteration rotates a plane by 2θ2\theta2θ, sin⁡θ=1/N\sin\theta=1/\sqrt Nsinθ=1/N​, and compute its success after ℓ\ellℓ iterations, a count at the first peak and the cost of overshooting.
  • Prove that a classical algorithm with bounded error needs at least 2N/3−12N/3-12N/3−1 queries.
  • Prove by the hybrid argument that every quantum algorithm with bounded error and any workspace needs at least (2N/3−1)/2(\sqrt{2N/3}-1)/2(2N/3​−1)/2 queries.
  • State the status of both bounds, and why “trying all answers at once” misdescribes the algorithm.

9.7 Status and the second answer

Both bounds are proved, unconditionally, in the query model: quantum search needs of the order of N\sqrt NN​ queries, classical search of the order of NNN (asking N−1N-1N−1 strings suffices). Like Simon’s (Lecture 5), the comparison is relative to an oracle; unlike it, the gap is quadratic.

It says nothing about problems given by a circuit, where an algorithm may read the circuit and the floor does not apply. To find the one input accepted by a known circuit, Grover’s algorithm makes about π4N\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N4π​N​ queries, each built from the circuit (Theorem 1.9), against up to N−1N-1N−1 runs for trying every input: a quadratic speed-up over exhaustive search, not over every classical algorithm. Whether NP\NPNP, the decision problems whose yes-instances have a certificate that a classical algorithm checks in polynomial time, lies in BQP\BQPBQP is open, and the floor decides nothing about it (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani, p. 3).

The course asks what makes the difference visible. Its second answer: the difference is interference arranged by structure, counted against a stated classical model, and bounded below. Simon’s algorithm and phase estimation arrange interference by a group; Grover’s by two reflections. The floor refutes “trying all answers at once”: one query on ∣u⟩\ket{u}∣u⟩ writes every value of fff into a phase, yet a reading after it returns www with probability 1/N1/N1/N, as a guess does. The hybrid argument bounds what interference can do: on average over www, the distance from the run that sees no marked item grows by at most 2/N2/\sqrt N2/N​ per query.
