Lecture 8

Factoring

Lecture 7 read an eigenphase with a circuit. This lecture asks: how does a phase become a factor? Factoring reduces, by a gcd, to the order rrr of a random aaa modulo NNN (Proposition 8.2). Phase estimation of the multiplication by aaa, started in ∣1⟩\ket{1}∣1⟩, reads a fraction s/rs/rs/r, and continued fractions turn it into rrr (Theorem 8.9). Together they find a factor of an nnn-bit NNN with circuits of O(n3)O(n^{3})O(n3) gates (Theorem 8.4), where no classical algorithm of polynomial time is known.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Prove that gcd⁡(ar/2−1,N)\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,N)gcd(ar/2−1,N) is a proper factor of NNN when rrr is even and ar/2≢−1a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1ar/2≡−1, and state how often a random aaa qualifies.
  • Prove that ∣1⟩\ket{1}∣1⟩ is the uniform superposition of the eigenvectors of UaU_aUa​, and deduce what phase estimation reads on it.
  • Prove that 3n3n3n runs with t=2n+1t=2n+1t=2n+1 counting qubits, continued fractions and a classical test find rrr with probability at least 2/32/32/3.
  • State the status of factoring: what is proved, what is cited and what is conjectured.

8.1 The reduction, its gcd, preprocessing and probability

Factoring asks, for a composite NNN, for a proper factor: a divisor ddd of NNN with 1<d<N1<d<N1<d<N. In this lecture NNN is the number to factor, no longer 2n2^{n}2n as in Lecture 7; it has nnn bits. Two cases are classical and fast: an even NNN has the factor 222, and a power N=bkN=b^{k}N=bk, k≥2k\ge2k≥2, the factor bbb, the integer kkk-th root of NNN for some k<nk<nk<n (Nielsen and Chuang, Ex. 5.17). A prime NNN, which has none, is recognised in time polynomial in nnn by the Miller–Rabin test (Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi, §4.2). What remains is an odd NNN with at least two distinct prime factors.

Choose aaa uniformly in {1,…,N−1}\{1,\dots,N-1\}{1,…,N−1}. Euclid’s algorithm gives gcd⁡(a,N)\gcd(a,N)gcd(a,N) in time polynomial in nnn; if it is not 111, it is a proper factor. Extended, it gives integers uuu and vvv with ua+vN=gcd⁡(a,N)ua+vN=\gcd(a,N)ua+vN=gcd(a,N) (Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi, p. 40). So an aaa coprime to NNN, a unit modulo NNN, has an inverse uuu: ua≡1ua\equiv1ua≡1.

Definition 8.1 (The order). For aaa coprime to NNN, the order of aaa modulo NNN is the least r≥1r\ge1r≥1 with ar≡1(modN)a^{r}\equiv1\pmod Nar≡1(modN).

It exists, and r<Nr<Nr<N: the powers a0,…,aN−1a^{0},\dots,a^{N-1}a0,…,aN−1 are units, of which there are at most N−1N-1N−1, so two agree, ai≡aja^{i}\equiv a^{j}ai≡aj with i<ji<ji<j, and multiplying by the inverse of aia^{i}ai gives aj−i≡1a^{j-i}\equiv1aj−i≡1. (This rrr is not the Bloch vector of Quantum Mechanics.)

Proposition 8.2 (The reduction proved modulo extended Euclid). Let aaa be coprime to NNN, with order rrr. If rrr is even and ar/2≢−1(modN)a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod Nar/2≡−1(modN), then gcd⁡(ar/2−1,N)\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,N)gcd(ar/2−1,N) and gcd⁡(ar/2+1,N)\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,N)gcd(ar/2+1,N) are proper factors of NNN.

Proof. Let b=ar/2 mod Nb=a^{r/2}\bmod Nb=ar/2modN. Then NNN divides b2−1=(b−1)(b+1)b^{2}-1=(b-1)(b+1)b2−1=(b−1)(b+1). So 1<gcd⁡(b−1,N)<N1<\gcd(b-1,N)<N1<gcd(b−1,N)<N, and likewise for b+1b+1b+1. 

Theorem 8.3 (A random base cited). Let NNN be odd with m≥2m\ge2m≥2 distinct prime factors, and let aaa be uniform among the units modulo NNN. Then rrr is even and ar/2≢−1a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1ar/2≡−1 with probability at least 1−2−(m−1)≥121-2^{-(m-1)}\ge\tfrac121−2−(m−1)≥21​.

An aaa uniform in {1,…,N−1}\{1,\dots,N-1\}{1,…,N−1}, once it is coprime to NNN, is uniform among the units. It is Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi’s (pp. 121–122) and Shor’s (p. 16). Nielsen and Chuang’s Theorem 5.3 (p. 233) prints 1−2−m1-2^{-m}1−2−m, which is false: for N=21N=21N=21 the probability is exactly 12\tfrac1221​.

Theorem 8.4 is about a function problem, and states an algorithm with its resources: bounded error, O(n)O(n)O(n) circuits of O(n3)O(n^{3})O(n3) gates. It is proved modulo the cited lemmas. No classical algorithm of polynomial time is known; the best known, the number field sieve, runs in time exp⁡(O(n1/3(log⁡n)2/3))\exp(O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3}))exp(O(n1/3(logn)2/3)) by a heuristic analysis (Nielsen and Chuang, p. 216; de Wolf, p. 37). That none exists is a conjecture, on which much public-key cryptography rests. This is the evidence Lecture 6 promised for its conjecture BQP≠BPP\BQP\neq\BPPBQP=BPP: a quantum algorithm proved, a classical one not known. Unlike Simon’s separation (Lecture 5), it needs no oracle, but no classical lower bound is proved.

  • The phase: ∣1⟩\ket{1}∣1⟩ is the uniform superposition of the eigenvectors of UaU_aUa​, with the phases s/rs/rs/r (Lemma 8.5).
